# _Why Design is Hard_ by Scott Berkun and Bryan Zug {#why-design-is-hard}

_A sharp, practical guide to thriving as a designer inside any organization, with particular resonance for those working on society's toughest challenges._{.intro}

![Cover of Why Design is Hard by Scott Berkun and Bryan Zug](/images/content/illustration-book-why-design-is-hard-inline.jpg){.float-right}

Books should get more credit for being short and to-the-point. I flew through _[Why Design is Hard](https://scottberkun.com/why-design-is-hard/)_ in just two days. It packs a lot of great advice into 100 pages.

As you probably have already figured out in your design career, creating great designs is a surprisingly small part of the job. You can create excellent solutions, but if you don't have the influence and buy-in within your organization to ship those designs, it hardly matters. The authors do a great job breaking down where influence comes from in many organizations and how you can build up your own power to ship great design work.

## Selling good design is important {#selling-good-design-is-important}

The authors make a strong case that selling your design work is part of the design process. You might come up with the perfect solution in 10 minutes, but you'll likely spend the next 2 months getting everyone to agree that it's the right solution. To be a great designer, you need to be good at selling your work and you need to be excellent at working cross-functionally with everyone else in your organization to do great design work together.

The book also smartly makes a strong case for designers to leverage their skills as facilitators and prototypers to sell design and build design within their teams.

This matters in all organizations, but selling your design work is particularly important in organizations that work on thorny problems like climate change and healthcare. In these situations, you will work with many people who don't understand design and it is part of your job as a designer to explain why your design will work better to achieve the organization's aims. In my experience, this "selling good design" effort will take a lot of your time and will, in the end, pay off in spades.

For example, if you are on a team working on a public health program, there is a strong chance that few of your colleagues will understand why human-centered design is important. A lot of government officials or public health experts will jump to technical solutions. It's up to you to bring the voice of real users to the table and to advocate for their needs. It's up to you to show how design will benefit those users… and ultimately how this will benefit the overall health program. When you work on hard problems, this is all foundational to the designer's job.

## Know your powers {#know-your-powers}

This was my favorite part of the book. The authors look at where power lies in most organizations. For instance, generalists hold a lot of power. The CEO has the most responsibility (and the highest paycheck) because she has to know a lot about a lot of areas. To build your power as a designer, it certainly helps to grow your experience outside of design so you can collaborate better with everyone else.

For instance, I spent the last 8 years working in global public health. I am not an epidemiologist and I don't have an MPH (Masters in Public Health), but I did pick up a lot of knowledge about how public health programs run, how chronic disease programs get managed by governments, and how health technologies are developed. All of those skills significantly increased my power to ship top-tier design in a challenging environment.

![A simple sketch showing the overlap between designer and product manager decisions, with real power sitting at the intersection](/images/content/illustration-designer-pm-design-decisions.png){.normal-width}

I particularly love this simple sketch about where real power lies in between product managers and designers. Most senior designers that I know have gravitated towards product roles in order to be closer to where real power lives on their teams.

## Be a connector {#be-a-connector}

Don't get stuck in the design silo. Get out and get to know everyone else in your organization and leverage your design skills to create connections between teams. As the authors say, "organizations reward connectors." You will build your influence, incorporate good ideas from across the organization, and become invaluable.

Creating connections is a powerful function on teams where people have really different backgrounds. When I was working with a group of epidemiology experts to create tools for Ministries of Health to plan for disease outbreaks, we used a Design Sprint to bring epidemiologists, nurses, and government workers together to work on a common challenge. The sprint process means we all had to create shared goals, co-develop potential solutions, and prototype together. Of course, the actual design work was important, but the opportunity to connect the teams together to create alignment was the most effective part of the week.

## Short and sweet {#short-and-sweet}

I'll keep this book review pithy because the book itself is short-and-sweet. I highly recommend that you pick up a copy of _[Why Design is Hard](https://scottberkun.com/why-design-is-hard/)_ and implement the good ideas to benefit your own career.
