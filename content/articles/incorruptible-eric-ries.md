# _Incorruptible_ by Eric Ries {#incorruptible-by-eric-ries}

_The author of <em>The Lean Startup</em> has written a book about companies that actually have a higher purpose. It's shockingly good._{.intro}

![Cover of Incorruptible by Eric Ries](/images/content/incorruptible-eric-ries.jpg){.float-right}

I doubt he remembers it, but [Eric Ries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Ries) and I ate breakfast together way back in 2010. Over eggs at a cafe in Wellington, New Zealand, we had a great discussion about prototyping back before prototyping and testing your ideas was so obvious in the tech startup world.

These were very different times. I was the director of design at a social media pioneer called [Digg](https://digg.com) and he was in the midst of writing a book called _[The Lean Startup](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/10127019-the-lean-startup)_, which would go on to be a bestseller and influence startup founders for a generation who would copy his style of rapid prototype testing that we all now take for granted. Whether he intended it or not, _The Lean Startup_ was embraced by the move-fast-and-break-things entrepreneurs of Silicon Valley.

I honestly hadn't tracked Eric's work closely for the last 10 years, so it came as a bit of a shock to pick up his new book _[Incorruptible: Why Good Companies Go Bad and How Great Companies Stay Great](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/460881/incorruptible-by-ries-eric/9780241692028)_. This isn't a book about how to move fast and break things.

Don't let your eyes glaze over, but this is a book about setting your mission higher and then it's about \*gasp\* corporate governance. I think it's an excellent book that every entrepreneur or leader should read.

## Companies don't have to be typical {#companies-dont-have-to-be-typical}

We all know the typical company playbook. The founder identifies an opportunity in the market, they raise some money to hire a team, they build a product and iterate their way to product-market fit, and then they make millions of dollars growing their sales and fending off competitors.

I've always been skeptical that you must follow that playbook. When I co-founded the design agency [silverorange](https://silverorange.com) back in 1999 with five friends, we didn't want to maximize profit; we wanted to do great work that excited us. Don't get me wrong, we weren't working for free, but we chose to pay all of the team the same amount, we charged our clients fair rates so we could win great projects, and we did some free projects (like the [Firefox brand](https://hicks.design/journal/firefox-logo) identity) because we were passionate about open source. This focus on doing good work and paying employees fairly is part of the reason that silverorange is still in business 26 years later — much longer than the typical design/dev agency.

In _Incorruptible_, Eric tells many stories about companies that are much larger than our little design studio. Companies like pharmaceutical giant [Novo Nordisk](https://www.novonordisk.com/), health insurer [Devoted Health](https://www.devoted.com/), outdoor company [Patagonia](https://patagonia.com/), and retail giant [Costco](https://www.costco.com) are structured in ways that allow them to operate with a higher mission.

For instance, Patagonia has resisted pressures to operate as a typical retailer that maximizes sales. That's partly because its founder [Yvon Chouinard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yvon_Chouinard) is a passionate environmentalist but also because they intentionally structured the company so it doesn't need to operate in a traditional way. In 2022, Chouinard and his family gave up ownership entirely. Rather than sell it, go public, or pass it to their children, they transferred it to a trust and a nonprofit. The shares were split into two kinds:

- **Patagonia Purpose Trust** holds all the voting stock, about 2% of the total. Its only job is to protect the company's values and mission. The Chouinard family elects and oversees its trustees.
- **Holdfast Collective** is a nonprofit holding the other 98% of the shares. These are non-voting, so they carry economic value but no say in decisions.

Patagonia still makes a lot of money. Profit it doesn't reinvest goes as a dividend to the Holdfast Collective to fund environmental work, initially projected at about $100 million a year. The company's [slogan](https://eu.patagonia.com/gb/en/ownership/) for this is that "Earth is now our only shareholder." An independent "Protector" monitors and enforces the trust's purpose. If the company's board fails to remove a CEO who abuses their position, the Purpose Trust can do it.

## Companies don't have to go bad {#companies-dont-have-to-go-bad}

Even well-intentioned companies often go bad. Corporate boards often overrule founders or replace the founder with a new CEO, major shareholders make demands to change the company, and new leadership takes over and steers the company in new directions. A company that once had a strong mission ends up with a watered-down slogan splashed on corporate walls, but the company becomes just another profit-maximizing entity.

As a typical company succeeds (in terms of profit), it will inevitably drift toward short-term financial extraction. That is, unless you don't operate like a typical company.

> "The more golden the goose, the greater the temptation to butcher it."
>
> — Eric Ries

Eric tells the stories of many companies that go wrong, and their lessons are instructive. For instance, [Sol Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sol_Price) started a chain of discount department stores called [FedMart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FedMart) that not only sold well-priced goods to customers, but paid twice the going rate to its employees. Despite massive growth and over 40 successful stores, investors pushed Price out of the company he founded because he wasn't willing to compromise on employee wages to squeeze out higher margins. Price knew that his employees were the special advantage of FedMart, but his investors took the typical corporate playbook to grow profits. This short-term thinking ended up killing FedMart, which was out of business less than eight years later.

Importantly, Sol Price learned from his mistakes at FedMart and then went on to found Price Club, which merged with Costco, co-founded by his protégé Jim Sinegal. Obviously, there are things we can criticize about Costco, but the way that the company kept prices low while paying employees a strong wage is incredible for a massive American corporation.

> "Success alone will not protect what matters most. Only incorruptible design can."
>
> — Eric Ries, _Incorruptible_

The most interesting part of _Incorruptible_ is Eric's ideas for creating governance structures that avoid the type of corporate fate that befell FedMart. He leans on examples like Novo Nordisk and Patagonia to suggest structures like the "spiritual holding company" that separates two things a normal company combines: the profit-making business and the mission. Each goes into its own entity, and the mission entity controls the business.

Novo Nordisk is his founding example. In the 1920s, the Danish physicians who licensed insulin were worried that a for-profit company might one day charge whatever it liked for a drug like insulin. They put the company under a nonprofit foundation that oversees its scientific mission. That setup has lasted more than a century. Eric says the foundation once stepped in to stop the company liquidating itself, and claims that intervention created over $500 billion in shareholder value.

Eric also tells the stories of companies like Grundfos, IKEA, Bosch, Zeiss, Rolex, Patagonia, and Anthropic. His headline statistic is that such companies are six times more likely than the typical company to reach their 50th year.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

As I get older, I'm increasingly proud that our design agency, silverorange, is still in business and has done great work for 26 years. It's an employee-owned company, it operates ethically, and it has adapted well through 3 CEOs. This isn't just through good intentions. It's because we made governance decisions that have stood the test of time.

In _Incorruptible_ it's really helpful to read stories from large companies that buck the corporate trend to do more than squeeze out profit. It was exciting to see examples of companies much larger and under much more pressure than silverorange, that succeed with a mission.

It's rare for me to recommend a business book, but this is a strong exception. _Incorruptible_ is a thoughtful book that should inspire founders to want more from their companies than making millions.
