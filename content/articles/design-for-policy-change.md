# Design for policy change {#design-for-policy-change}

_Public policy is often the foundation for change that touches nearly every part of our lives. How can designers play a role in better policy-making?_{.intro}

For a long time, the word "policy" felt completely abstract to me. The idea that a public body bore direct responsibility for designing and maintaining everyday systems was not a part of how I processed the world around me. I simply built a workaround and moved on, like everyone else — fuel for the generator when the power went out, a water man hauling jerry cans when the taps ran dry, a levy for the men who watched the gate at night.

Last December, a system outage at the international airport in Abuja, Nigeria — the staff simply told us there was "no network" — brought operations to a standstill. Airline staff stood paralyzed behind frozen monitors, unable to process connecting flights or communicate what was happening to us. Crisis communication broke down because customer support, and everything else, depended on internet connectivity. No wayfinding signage, no spatial cues to help people through the building, no nondigital way to understand anything.

It took an increasingly furious crowd of delayed passengers to force staff into manually writing out boarding passes with pen and paper in hurried, muddled handwriting.

To compound the chaos, the airport offered no public Wi-Fi, so international travelers like me had no internet. I was left completely disconnected, forced to beg strangers for hotspot access just to send a message to my mom, waiting at a different terminal building. She spent four extra hours unable to talk to me because the airport had no backup for when the network went down.

I saw what happens when the systems around us are not designed with failure in mind. And where policies can help.

## So, what is policy? {#so-what-is-policy}

Public policy is how a government works through a problem that affects lots of people. For instance, whether everyone can afford to see a doctor or whether factories can pump carbon into the air.

In theory, it works like this. A problem gets noticed, and enough people care that leaders have to respond. Researchers weigh the options and the trade-offs. Elected officials debate and vote on one. Agencies put it into action, like capping how much pollution a power plant can release. Then someone checks whether it's working, and if it isn't, the policy gets adjusted or scrapped.

In practice, it's far messier. A problem often has to become politically expensive before anyone moves on it. The solution that gets picked is often already available, or one someone has been pushing for years, which finally creates an opening. Timing belongs to election cycles, leaders' pet interests, scandals, and whatever the public is angry about this month. And the check at the end often doesn't happen at all.

## What problems can design help to solve? {#what-problems-can-design-help-to-solve}

I only started thinking about any of this after I moved to the U.K. and got a job showing local council governments how to use maps to back up the decisions they were making. Public health teams, GIS analysts, and anyone else who needed to know which streets in their area were struggling and why. It was the first time I saw governance as something built and maintained and not something that simply happened to me.

But even here, with good data and money behind it, something was still going wrong. Policy-making is never going to be tidy, but some of that mess is avoidable. Rules get made before anyone checks what they do to real people. Design can move that check earlier.

Take averages, for instance. Policy is usually written around them, which can hide people. A neighborhood can look statistically healthy on paper while specific streets within it still lack basic services. A facility can sit inside one statistical area on the map while the people it serves come from several. Measure only the aggregate, and the reality of the people underneath it disappears.

Over time, this produces what political scientists call policy drift: the rule stays the same on paper while the world it was written for has moved on.

Design can help close that gap, but not in the way people usually assume. It is not a final layer of polish, applied once decisions are already made. It can shape how a rule is delivered, help build it alongside the people writing it, or challenge it with outside evidence when it's failing.

## Where to engage with design? {#where-to-engage-with-design}

![Illustration of a woman in glasses pointing at a chart on a presentation board](/images/content/illustration-design-for-policy.png){.float-right}

To understand how design currently interacts with policy, it helps to look at it across three levels of engagement:

- **Level 1 is sensemaking:** Taking something the public can't read (a dense budget, a complicated statute or a spreadsheet of local statistics) and turning it into something they can understand. The state is made visible through information design (e.g., charts, dashboards, maps, or infographics).
- **Level 2 is friction mapping:** Finding out what is happening to the people experiencing a policy. It combines the quantitative evidence of spatial analysis, for example, with qualitative user research. Together, they can shift the evidence from one abstract aggregate (attendance is low in this district) to a specific human context (families have to walk ninety minutes to a school that opens before the buses run).
- **Level 3 is prototyping:** Testing a policy as a live service before and during implementation, ensuring the human workflow functions before any rules are codified into law.

Where an ecosystem operates on this spectrum reflects its institutional maturity.

For the past few years, I worked in the U.K. in product operations and gathering customer insight for a spatial data platform. I worked mostly at Levels 1 and 2: guiding teams through a spatial platform, helping them visualize deprivation, map health boundaries, and generate the profile reports that back their policy choices. The platform provided the evidence; what they then do with it happens further down the line in roles and practices that formally exist in the U.K. These include recognized Civil Service cadres for service designers and user researchers, mandatory GDS standards, and unified design systems. That handoff from sensemaking to the research and friction mapping that should follow doesn't always happen, and the cost scales.

Take LSOAs, for instance. On the map, a facility can sit inside one Lower Super Output Area (LSOA) — the small statistical unit funding eligibility is usually assessed by in the U.K. — while the families using it come from several surrounding ones. The map is accurate, but it can't answer questions about people using a boundary meant for geography.

On a national scale, Universal Credit highlighted the same gap. It calculated entitlement on income received within a fixed monthly window, which means that if your payday changed because of a weekend or a bank holiday, two salary payments could come within one window and sharply cut, or even zero out, your award for that month. Four claimants [took the DWP](https://www.gov.uk/administrative-appeals-tribunal-decisions/jn-v-secretary-of-state-for-work-and-pensions-uc) (Department for Work and Pensions) to the Court of Appeal in 2020 and won; the court found the approach irrational, and the rule was eventually amended. But it had already been written, legislated, and rolled out to millions before it was tested against a dozen real payslips.

Design also has not fully settled into the U.K. system. The U.K. government has a training market to help designers and researchers win permission to do the work. There are courses on influencing decision makers to fund user-centered design, on responding to arguments against it, or on getting design into governance and funding conversations. The standards exist, but the practice still has to be argued for.

## What about in lower-income countries? {#what-about-in-lower-income-countries}

In places like Nigeria, the problem is different. Design and data capability also exists, but mostly outside government. We see this heavily in the work of organizations like BudgIT, alongside data journalism and research platforms like Dataphyte and Stears that translate opaque economic and policy data into public intelligence. This ecosystem remains deeply valuable, but it operates mostly as an outside-in vehicle for Level 1 sensemaking through information design. When the state does move from Level 1, it frequently attempts to skip from paper to code. Governments rush straight to software deployment and launching portals.

Just before my mom retired from public service, I watched her navigate a massive wave of institutional digital transformation: new software, digitized records, changed workflows, all arriving fully formed.

That is changing, though slowly. The Nigerian Ministry of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy is bringing software developers and civil servants into the same room, while organizations like [BRAGI](https://www.bragi.digital/) and [Public Tech Studio](https://publictech.studio/) deploy design and data methodologies directly into public service delivery across Africa.

## Many policies are never tested {#many-policies-are-never-tested}

Most execution failures were built into a policy before it was tested.

A committee drafts a document, lawmakers vote, and the regulation or law is passed down to civil servants to make it work. The assumption is that a rule written in an office will hold up once it gets to real people, and it often doesn't.

[Cyd Harrell](https://cydharrell.com/), in her excellent book [_A Civic Technologist's Practice Guide_](https://digitalgovernmenthub.org/library/a-civic-technologists-practice-guide/), explains why governments move slowly. Public bodies are not judged on profit. They're judged on whether they spent public money fairly and well, which makes them cautious.

Level 3 Prototyping does not fight this caution; it serves it. Testing a rule on 50 people for two weeks is a cheaper way of being careful than finding the flaw after a national rollout. And it doesn't always need a platform, just sitting with people and watching where they're getting stuck.

When decision-makers do embrace design, they frequently default to digital solutionism — the assumption that the answer to every policy challenge must be some app, a portal, or a digital repository.

Having lived in both the U.K. and Nigeria, I have seen that dependency fail in two ways: by shutting out people who cannot get online, or by leaving everyone stranded when internet connectivity goes down.

In the U.K., the push toward "digital by default" public services such as online-only welfare access locks out the elderly, neurodivergent, or low-income citizens who cannot afford reliable internet or are not confident using it. In Nigeria, I saw this exact thing happen at the airport. An entire system was running on a single network with no backup for when it went down.

This is why human-centered public design must look beyond screens and value some form of analog resilience.

This is not an argument against digital tools. It's one for keeping physical ones, and sometimes the physical version is the better tool outright. A remarkable example of this is [Finland's Osallistumispeli (The Participation Game)](https://www.peoplepowered.org/resources-content/the-participation-game) — a physical board game designed for city officials and residents who sit at a table to visually negotiate urban policy and budget trade-offs together, making abstract civic choices tangible before any legislation is drafted. Digital tools should expand access, not hold basic public life hostage. Whether in Helsinki, London, or Abuja, the measure of a good service isn't how modern it looks on a good day, but how humanely it treats a citizen.

## Why is design often overlooked by policymakers? {#why-is-design-often-overlooked-by-policymakers}

In Nigeria and across similar administrative states, there seem to exist critical barriers to the meaningful integration of design in policy change. My read of the situation is that there are no formal procurement pathways for service design, no shared vocabulary between civil servants and service designers, no cross-silo collaboration, and success metrics that reward delivery speed over real human benefit. Also, when donor funding mandates prepackaged technical solutions instead of open-ended problem framing, design is locked out before the project begins.

Past attempts to address friction — like the creation of [SERVICOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SERVICOM) in Nigeria in 2004 — treated service failure as a matter of customer service compliance rather than strategic policy design. Today, as agencies like Galaxy Backbone and NITDA drive an aggressive push toward paperless, automated state infrastructure, the responsibility for system design falls almost entirely to traditional IT officers and program analysts.

So institutional authority is granted almost exclusively to builders of "hard" infrastructure — software vendors, tech founders, and engineering teams. A recent example among many is Nigeria's [DevsInGovernment](https://fmcide.gov.ng/dr-tijani-launches-devsingovernment-to-improve-use-of-technology-in-the-nigerian-civil-service/). The convening is genuinely valuable, and I have already named it as a sign of change — but bringing developers into government is not the same as bringing designers, and across the broader civic tech ecosystem, well-meaning open-source developers regularly flood the web with civic portals built without service designers.

There's a risk to building tools that gradually become forgotten digital monuments rather than functional public design. Even when these teams include UI/UX or product designers, their mandate is confined to screen delivery. What remains missing is the autonomy for designers to prototype and stress-test nondigital touchpoints, low-fidelity digital MVPs, admin friction, and physical counters before a single line of code is ever written.

Global precedents show that alternative models do exist. Across Latin America, initiatives like Chile's [Laboratorio de Gobierno](https://participedia.net/case/5971) bypassed rigid civil service cadres by creating autonomous government labs to hire service designers and user researchers directly onto policy pipelines. Similar design-led frameworks in Kenya and South Africa have proven that user-centered design can streamline public transit and civic workflows without forcing design into outdated boxes.

![A workshop at Chile's Laboratorio de Gobierno: government officials and members of the public sit at round tables working through printed materials while a facilitator stands beside a projected countdown timer](/images/content/photo-chile-laboratorio-de-gobierno.jpg){.normal-width}
_The [Government Lab](https://participedia.net/case/5971) of Chile, where state officials and representatives of civil society come together with the common objective of promoting a modern and innovative state at the service of citizens._{.caption}

Until governments in Nigeria and across the region build the structural pathways that make design legible — adapting procurement codes beyond rigid IT and printing tenders, creating formal civil service roles for service designers/user researchers, and measuring human outcomes — design will remain locked outside the decision, reduced to polish on policy that has already been written.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

Good design in policy is one of the lowest-cost, highest-impact things a government can do. Finding billions for a health service is hard. Redesigning a form, fixing a confusing verification rule, or building in a backup for when a network fails costs almost nothing by comparison.

The barrier isn't a lack of evidence. Most departments have too little incentive to look for better design: roles are narrow, budgets are judged on whether they were spent rather than what they achieved. Designers pushing for this work are swimming against that tide.

If you're a designer wondering where you fit in policy work, the opening isn't at the end, after the rule is written and the rollout has failed. It's earlier. Design is most effective in the rooms where problems are still being defined and solutions are being debated. That's where the seat at the table is often empty and needs filling by skilled, pragmatic designers.

Design for policy change is one of the most high-leverage opportunities for the right designer.
