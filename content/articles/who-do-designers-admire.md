# Who do designers admire? It’s a surprisingly complicated question. {#who-do-designers-admire}

_Many design leaders that I meet can’t answer the question. I think that’s problematic._{.intro}

Last year, I did a keynote talk at the [Leading Design](https://leadingdesign.com/) conference in London. The night before the talk, the speakers were invited to a beautiful dinner at the [Barbican Centre](https://www.barbican.org.uk/) where we ate canapés with design leaders from places like The New York Times, Adobe, and GitHub.

There must have been about 18 people around the dinner table. At one point, I asked these design leaders, "Who do you admire in our industry?" or "What projects do you see that you admire?" It was so quiet that you could hear a pin drop. Literally no one at the table had an answer from recent history. There were a few mentions of the Bauhaus movement or designers like Paul Rand or Jony Ive, but, even when I pushed a bit uncomfortably, no one could give an answer from the last decade.

This isn't a one-off observation. Over the last year, I've been asking a lot of designers the same question. Just last week, I was at an event where a design luminary was interviewed on stage. An audience member asked her who she admires, and she literally said, "I'm a bit embarrassed to not have a good answer to that question. I'm sure I'll think of an answer at 2 a.m."

It's fine to not have "heroes" in design. Individual achievement isn't a great measure of design when most great design is the result of teamwork. But it feels problematic to me that most design leaders that I talk to can't even describe a recent project that they think is an example of great design.

Why is this a problem? I think it's important that we know good design when we see it. How will we know if our own work is good if we can't compare it to the best in our industry?

## Architects do better {#architects-do-better}

![Illustration of a person leaning over a tabletop model of a town](/images/content/illustration-architecture.svg){.float-right}

If you had a room full of architects and asked them the same question, "Who do you admire in our industry?", the room would spark to life with a robust debate.

Most architects would be able to tell you what recent projects were inventive, what ideas they might borrow from that project, who was behind it, and what shortcomings the project might have. Architects aren't just more engaged, but they have industry publications that they read, critics that they follow, and required continuing education programs that mean they're up-to-date on what's going on across their industry.

Architecture as a culture also has groups that establish cultural standards. I had lunch recently with a friend who is an excellent architect. He has served on juries for architectural awards. He pointed out to me that many architecture prizes have shifted their focus from "exciting" new buildings to well-considered renovated buildings or reuse of old structures — leaders in their field discussed what architects value and decided to emphasize more climate-friendly designs. Where is this type of debate in our design community? Where is this type of leadership?

Arguably, designers are very different from architects in important ways. For instance, there isn't a standard education for many design fields and there certainly aren't credentialing institutions that set a standard of professionalism or insist on continuing education. But I do think there are important things we can borrow from professions like architecture.

## What's needed? {#whats-needed}

What's missing? I'd love to [hear your own thoughts](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/dburka_who-do-designers-admire-often-when-i-ask-share-7531380508702740480-LvRb/) on this, but I think that we're really missing design criticism, real design conversations, community, design work in public, and a sense of purpose. Where are the writers who can articulate what's good and bad about work that's being produced? Where are the communities outside giant corporations like Figma and Adobe? RIP, the groups that were doing this like IxDA and the Index Awards.

### Critics {#critics}

I'd love to see designers who step up to write more critically about projects in our industry. We currently see more critical writing about luxury furniture design than we do about digital design that affects almost everyone on earth. People like [Pavel Samsonov](https://pavelsamsonov.com/) have been pushing ideas like this for years and I miss places like [A List Apart](https://alistapart.com/) where some of this used to live online.

</div>

<div class="content-cell">

![](/images/content/illustration-public.svg){.cell-illustration}

### Work in public {#work-in-public}

When I got into design in the early 2000s, there was a ton of design work and thinking that was publicly accessible. From [Dan Cederholm's](https://simplebits.com/about/) work in web standards, to [CSS Zen Garden](https://csszengarden.com/), to [Presstube](https://presstube.com/), to [37better](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/1546-patterns-great-self-promotion), there was a lot of online debate about the best way to do things. With the increasing corporatization of design, less of the work we do is exposed. I really hope that in the age of AI and more side projects, we start to work in public more.

</div>

<div class="content-cell">

![](/images/content/illustration-events.svg){.cell-illustration}

### Events {#events}

We need gatherings that aren't captured by corporate interests where we have robust debates about what we mean by good design. Figma Config is a great conference in many ways, but it's not a venue for the critical conversations we need to have about design, because it's fundamentally created to sell software. The old groups that organized these events like IxDA, AIGA, or the Index Awards, are either out of business or have lost their relevance.

</div>

<div class="content-cell">

![](/images/content/illustration-conversations.svg){.cell-illustration}

### Better conversations {#better-conversations}

Online or in-person, I think we should have more conversation about what good design is. Looking back at the history of design, what makes today's design the same or different? What do we see as highly relevant today? Who is pushing the boundaries of design that we respect? We don't all need to come to the same answers, but we need to bounce our thoughts off of each other to strengthen the culture of design.

</div>

</div>

We will try to do parts of this as Hard Problems, but we need many groups working towards these goals if we want design to thrive.

This gives you an idea of designers that we admire. As one of my co-founders, [Mahima Chandak](/authors/mahima-chandak), says "Good design solves problems. Great design solves problems that matter." These designers work on critical problems, in areas that rarely see great design, and they stick with it for a long time even when it's difficult. And, of course, they deliver incredible work. This is how design can positively impact the world.
